Every month a space sits empty costs rent, carrying costs, and momentum. Preparation will not change the market, but it can shorten vacancy and improve the terms you negotiate.

Whether the vacancy is a storefront, an office suite, a flex unit, or a warehouse in Reno or Sparks, prospective tenants ask the same basic questions: will this space work for my business, what will it really cost, and how quickly can I move in? Preparing clear answers before marketing begins puts the owner in a stronger position.

## Know who the space is for

Start by identifying the most likely tenant types for the space, based on its size, layout, zoning, utilities, parking, and location. Marketing a space to everyone usually means it speaks clearly to no one. A space with grade-level doors and a small office may suit a contractor or service company; a corner storefront with a hood may suit a restaurant.

## Get the specifications right

Inaccurate or incomplete specs waste tours and erode trust. Confirm and document:

- Rentable and usable area, and how they were measured
- Clear height, door counts and types, and power service, for industrial and flex space
- HVAC, restrooms, plumbing, and any specialized infrastructure
- Parking, signage rights, and access
- Zoning and the uses it allows

## Make operating costs easy to understand

Tenants increasingly compare total occupancy cost, not just base rent. Have a clear estimate of operating expenses ready, explain what is included, and be prepared to share recent history. Uncertainty about costs tends to be priced in by prospects. [What Does NNN Mean in a Commercial Lease?](/insights/what-does-nnn-mean-commercial-lease/) covers the questions tenants are likely to ask.

## Address the condition issues prospects will notice

You do not need to fully build out a space for an unknown tenant, but obvious problems make it harder for prospects to see potential. Consider:

- Cleaning, fresh paint, and working lighting
- Repairing visible damage, leaks, or stains
- Confirming HVAC, doors, and restrooms are in working order
- Removing a prior tenant's leftover fixtures or signage
- Improving curb appeal, landscaping, and parking-lot condition

## Decide how flexible you can be

Know your range on rent, term, improvement allowances, free rent, and who handles construction before negotiations start. Flexibility on one item can sometimes be traded for strength on another. A tenant with good credit and a longer term may justify a larger allowance, for example.

## Present the space clearly

- Good photos, a floor plan, and a concise property summary
- Accurate specs and estimated operating costs
- Notes on what improvements the owner may consider
- Easy access for tours and quick, clear answers to questions

## Track and adjust

Pay attention to who tours, what they ask, and why they pass. If several prospects raise the same concern, such as price, layout, or a missing feature, that feedback is valuable. Adjusting early is usually less costly than waiting months for the market to change.

## How I approach it

Before I market a space, I want three things: accurate specs, a clear operating-cost estimate, and a short list of the most likely tenant types. Those answer most of the questions prospects ask on the first call.

## Common questions

### Should I build out a space before I have a tenant?

Usually not fully, because different tenants need different layouts. Targeted improvements that help most likely tenants, such as working restrooms, updated lighting, or a clean shell, can be worth considering.

### Is it better to lower the rent or offer concessions?

It depends on your goals and financing. Concessions such as free rent or allowances can preserve the stated rent, which may matter for value, while a lower rent may attract more prospects. Compare the options over the full lease term.

### How do I know if my asking rent is right?

Look at verified leasing activity for comparable spaces and at how prospects respond. Consistent feedback about price, or very few tours, is a signal worth taking seriously.

### Practical takeaway

Know your likely tenant, document accurate specs and costs, fix what prospects will notice, and decide your flexibility before the first negotiation.
