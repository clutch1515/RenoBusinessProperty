Before a client signs a lease, I want to know one thing for certain: is the business allowed to operate at that address? A commercial building is not automatically approved for every commercial activity.

The problem usually shows up the same way: the space looks right, the lease gets signed, and then the business learns it needs a permit, an upgrade, or an approval that takes longer or costs more than expected. Verifying the use early protects both the timeline and the budget.

## Describe the actual operation

Zoning and permitting decisions depend on what actually happens in the space, so start with a clear written description of the business.

- Products or services offered
- Customer and employee activity, including peak counts
- Hours of operation
- Deliveries, loading, storage, or outdoor activity
- Food preparation, alcohol, medical, childcare, automotive, or other regulated activities
- Planned construction, equipment, signage, and utility needs

A short, accurate description makes conversations with planning staff, landlords, and design professionals faster and more productive.

## Confirm the jurisdiction

A Reno mailing address may not always mean the property is inside the City of Reno. Requirements can differ among Reno, Sparks, Washoe County, and nearby jurisdictions such as Storey County and Carson City. Confirm which agency governs the parcel before relying on any code research.

## Understand what zoning answers, and what it does not

Zoning determines whether a use is allowed in a district, and whether it is allowed outright, allowed with conditions, or requires a discretionary approval. It may also affect parking, signage, outdoor activity, and hours. Zoning does not answer every question, though. A use can be permitted by zoning and still require building, fire, or health approvals before it can open.

## Look at the building, not just the zoning map

A change in use can trigger building and fire code requirements even when zoning allows the business. Items that often come up include:

- Occupancy classification and the number of people the space is designed for
- Exits, fire separation, and fire sprinkler or alarm requirements
- Accessibility for customers and employees
- Restroom counts and plumbing capacity
- Ventilation, exhaust, and electrical capacity for specialized equipment

A design professional or contractor familiar with local code can help estimate whether the space needs work before the use can be approved.

## Licensing and other approvals

Most businesses need a local business license in addition to the state business license administered through the Nevada Secretary of State. Some uses also need health, liquor, or other specialized permits. The City of Reno states that commercial locations are reviewed for zoning compatibility and may require additional permitting. [Review the City of Reno Business Licensing Guide](https://www.reno.gov/business-development/business-licensing/business-license-guide.php) and the equivalent resources for the governing jurisdiction.

## Verify before making the lease unconditional

Start with the appropriate planning and licensing agencies, ask specific questions about the exact address and use, and document what you learn, including who you spoke with and when. Written confirmation is more reliable than a phone conversation, and a prior tenant’s similar use is not proof that your business will be approved.

## Coordinate the lease and approvals

Consider whether the lease should address permit timing, landlord cooperation with applications, who pays for required improvements, signage approvals, opening deadlines, and what happens if required approvals cannot be obtained. These points are easier to negotiate at the [letter of intent](/insights/commercial-real-estate-letter-of-intent-explained/) stage than after the lease is drafted.

## How I approach it

I make use verification part of the search, not an afterthought. We describe the operation in writing, confirm which jurisdiction governs the address, and get answers from the right agency before the lease becomes binding.

## Common questions

### If the previous tenant ran a similar business, am I approved?

Not necessarily. Approvals can lapse, codes change, and small differences in a use can matter. Verify your specific use at the specific address.

### When should I start checking zoning?

Before making an offer or signing a letter of intent, and certainly before the lease becomes binding. Early research costs little and can prevent an expensive mistake.

### Who should I talk to first?

Start with the planning or zoning staff of the jurisdiction that governs the parcel, then confirm licensing and building requirements. A broker can help organize the questions, and an attorney should review how approvals are handled in the lease.

### Practical takeaway

Do not treat a listing or a prior tenant’s use as approval for your business. Verify it directly, in writing, before you commit.
