When someone sends me a listing quoted “NNN,” my first question is simple: what will this space actually cost each month? The abbreviation is easy. The economics behind it take a few more questions.

Triple-net pricing is common for [retail](/retail-storefronts/), [flex](/flex-warehouse-space/), and many office properties in Reno, Sparks, and the rest of Northern Nevada. Two NNN quotes can describe very different obligations, so I treat the number in a listing as the start of the conversation, not the cost of the space.

## What the three “nets” usually refer to

In general use, the three nets are property taxes, property insurance, and common-area maintenance (often shortened to CAM). Under a triple-net structure, the landlord passes these costs through to tenants, usually in proportion to each tenant’s share of the building or project.

- **Property taxes:** the tenant’s share of the real property tax bill and any special assessments the lease allows the landlord to pass through.
- **Insurance:** the tenant’s share of the landlord’s property and liability coverage for the building and common areas.
- **Common-area maintenance:** shared costs such as parking-lot upkeep, landscaping, lighting, snow removal, trash, security, and property management.

Tenants typically carry their own business insurance, utilities, and interior maintenance in addition to these charges. Who handles the roof, structure, HVAC, and parking-lot replacement varies widely and should be confirmed in the lease rather than assumed from the label.

## NNN compared with gross and modified gross leases

Lease labels are used inconsistently in the market, so it helps to focus on which party pays each cost rather than on the name.

- **Full-service or gross:** the landlord pays most operating costs and builds them into the rent. Many gross leases still pass through increases above a base year or expense stop.
- **Modified gross:** a middle ground where some operating costs are included and others are paid separately. The split is defined by the lease, not by the term.
- **NNN:** the tenant pays base rent plus a share of taxes, insurance, and common-area costs. Some leases labeled NNN still leave structural items with the landlord; others do not.

## Base rent is only the starting point

A listing may quote base rent per square foot per month or per year. Confirm the unit, the rentable area used in the calculation, the commencement date, and the scheduled increases. Then add the estimated NNN charges, which are usually quoted separately and are estimates rather than guarantees.

## Ask what the additional charges include

- Property taxes and assessments
- Property and liability insurance allocations
- Common-area maintenance and management
- Landscaping, lighting, security, snow removal, and trash
- Capital repairs or replacements, where permitted by the lease
- Administrative or management fees and how they are calculated

## Understand reconciliation

Most NNN leases collect estimated monthly charges and then compare those estimates with actual costs after the year ends. If actual costs were higher, the tenant may owe a balance; if lower, the tenant may receive a credit. Ask how and when reconciliations are delivered, what supporting detail is provided, and how long the tenant has to question them.

## Review estimates, history, and limits

Current estimates may not show the full future exposure. Ask for recent reconciliations and budgets, then read the lease provisions that control the charges.

- **Exclusions:** costs the landlord cannot pass through, such as certain capital improvements, financing costs, or costs caused by another tenant.
- **Caps:** limits on how much certain controllable expenses can increase each year.
- **Audit rights:** the tenant’s ability to review the landlord’s records and the process for resolving errors.
- **Vacancy treatment:** how costs are allocated when part of the property is empty.
- **Capital items:** whether major replacements are passed through all at once, spread over their useful life, or excluded.

## Compare spaces on the same basis

Convert every serious option into an estimated total monthly and annual occupancy cost. Then add improvements, utilities, maintenance responsibilities, moving costs, and operational differences. A slightly higher base rent with clearer, more predictable charges can be the better decision. ## Questions for owners using NNN pricing

Property owners benefit from clear NNN structures too. Accurate budgets, timely reconciliations, and well-drafted exclusions reduce disputes and make a vacancy easier to lease. If operating costs are hard to explain, prospective tenants tend to discount the space accordingly. More owner guidance is on the [Property Owners](/property-owners/) page.

## How I approach it

When I help a tenant compare NNN spaces, I ask for the last two years of reconciliations and the current budget, then build an estimated total monthly cost for each option. It is usually the fastest way to see which space is really less expensive.

## Common questions

### Is NNN rent the same as total rent?

No. NNN rent usually refers to base rent only. Taxes, insurance, and common-area costs are added on top, and utilities and interior expenses are typically separate as well.

### Can NNN charges go up during the lease?

Yes. Because the charges reflect actual operating costs, they can rise or fall over the term. Caps, exclusions, and audit rights determine how much protection the tenant has.

### Who pays for a new roof under an NNN lease?

It depends on the lease. Some leases keep roof and structure with the landlord, some pass replacement costs through over time, and some place the obligation on the tenant. Confirm the language with qualified counsel before signing.

### Practical takeaway

A lower base rent can still be the more expensive space. Compare the full cost and the operational fit before you get attached to a number.
