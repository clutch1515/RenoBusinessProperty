When a client narrows the search to two or three spaces, I set the asking rents aside for a moment. They are the easiest numbers to compare and the most likely to mislead.

The spaces may be measured differently, carry different operating costs, need different improvements, and support the business in very different ways. A useful comparison puts every serious option on the same financial and operational footing.

## Start with total occupancy cost

Base rent may be quoted by the month or by the square foot per year. Depending on the lease structure, the tenant may also pay some combination of property taxes, insurance, common-area maintenance, utilities, repairs, and management costs. Ask what is included, what is estimated, and how those charges have changed. [What Does NNN Mean in a Commercial Lease?](/insights/what-does-nnn-mean-commercial-lease/) explains the most common structure.

## Check how the space is measured

Rent is usually charged on rentable square feet, which can include a share of common areas such as lobbies, corridors, and restrooms. Usable square feet is the area the business actually occupies. Two spaces with the same rentable size can offer noticeably different usable areas, so ask for both figures and how they were calculated.

## Account for the cost of making the space usable

- Design and permitting
- Construction and tenant improvements
- Furniture, fixtures, equipment, data, and signage
- Moving expense and operational downtime
- Restoration obligations at the end of the lease

A second-generation space that already fits the use may cost less to occupy than a lower-rent shell that needs a full build-out, even after a landlord allowance.

## Compare concessions over the full term

Free rent, improvement allowances, stepped rent, and options can materially change the economics. Spread those items across the same analysis period rather than letting one headline concession drive the decision. A common approach is to total all expected costs over the term, subtract the value of concessions, and divide by the number of months and square feet to get an effective cost that can be compared across options.

## Price the operational fit

A lower rent can be expensive if the site creates delivery problems, limits signage, lacks parking, needs more staffing, or cannot support the intended use. Compare access, layout, utilities, loading, visibility, and customer or employee convenience alongside the lease figures. For property-type specifics, see the [retail site selection checklist](/insights/retail-site-selection-checklist-reno-sparks/) and the [warehouse and flex requirements checklist](/insights/warehouse-flex-space-requirements-checklist/).

## Weigh flexibility and risk

Term length, renewal and expansion options, termination rights, assignment and subletting provisions, and the personal guarantee can matter as much as price. A shorter term or a right to expand may be worth paying for if the business is growing or its future needs are uncertain.

## Use a simple comparison table

- Usable and rentable area
- Base rent and escalation schedule
- Operating expense estimate and what it includes
- Upfront improvements and moving cost
- Concessions and landlord contribution
- Term, options, and exit flexibility
- Major operational advantages and constraints

Filling in the same rows for every option makes trade-offs visible and keeps the conversation focused on facts rather than first impressions.

## How I approach it

I build a simple side-by-side for every finalist: the same rows, the same term, the same assumptions. Once the numbers are laid out that way, the right choice is usually easier to see, and easier to negotiate.

## Common questions

### Should I always choose the space with the lowest effective cost?

Not automatically. The best choice supports the business at an acceptable total cost and risk. A slightly more expensive space with better access or a better layout can produce more revenue or lower operating costs.

### How many spaces should I compare in detail?

Usually two or three serious finalists. Comparing too many options in detail slows the process; comparing only one removes negotiating leverage.

### When should I involve an architect or contractor?

Before choosing between finalists that need meaningful improvements. A preliminary cost estimate can change which option is actually less expensive.

### Plain-English takeaway

The best value is the space that supports your business at an acceptable total cost and risk, not automatically the one with the lowest asking rent.
