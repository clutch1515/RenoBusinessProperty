A capitalization rate, or cap rate, is one of the most common terms in commercial real estate. I find it a useful shorthand, because it connects income to value in one percentage, but it is also one of the easiest numbers to misuse.

## The basic formula

A cap rate is a property's annual net operating income divided by its value or price.

**Cap rate = Net operating income ÷ Value**

Turned around, the same relationship estimates value from income: **Value = Net operating income ÷ Cap rate**.

For example, a property with $100,000 of net operating income that sells for $2,000,000 has a cap rate of 5 percent. *These numbers illustrate the arithmetic only. They are not a Reno or Northern Nevada market figure.*

## What net operating income includes

Net operating income, or NOI, is the property's income after operating expenses but before loan payments, income taxes, and depreciation.

- **Included:** rent and other property income, minus operating expenses such as taxes, insurance, maintenance, utilities the owner pays, and management.
- **Not included:** mortgage payments, the owner's income taxes, and depreciation.

Because NOI drives the result, it matters whether the figure reflects current actual income, a stabilized estimate, or a projection. Always ask which one you are looking at.

## What a cap rate tells you

A cap rate roughly reflects the return a buyer expects from the property's income, given its risk. In general, a lower cap rate means buyers are paying more for each dollar of income, often because they see the income as more secure or likely to grow. A higher cap rate usually signals more perceived risk or less growth.

## What moves cap rates

- Tenant strength and remaining lease term
- Property type, age, and condition
- Location and access
- Interest rates and the broader investment market
- Expected rent growth and vacancy risk

## Where cap rates are misused

- **Comparing unlike properties.** A new building with a long lease to a strong tenant and an older building with short leases should not be expected to share a cap rate.
- **Mixing actual and projected income.** A cap rate based on optimistic projected rent is not comparable to one based on actual income.
- **Ignoring capital needs.** A property needing a new roof may show attractive income today, but a buyer will account for that cost.
- **Applying it to owner-user buildings.** Buildings typically sold to businesses that will occupy them are often valued more by comparable sales than by income.

## How owners can use cap rates well

Use cap rates as one lens alongside comparable sales and an understanding of your property's leases and condition. When someone quotes a market cap rate, ask what kinds of properties it reflects, how current the evidence is, and whether the income behind it was actual or projected. [Commercial Property Values](/commercial-property-values/) explains the broader approaches to value.

## How I approach it

When someone quotes me a cap rate, I ask three questions: what kinds of properties is it based on, how current is the evidence, and was the income actual or projected? The answers tell you whether the number applies to your property at all.

## Common questions

### Is a higher cap rate better?

It depends on your perspective. For a buyer, a higher cap rate means more income per dollar invested, usually with more risk. For a seller, a lower cap rate on the same income means a higher price.

### Does a cap rate include my mortgage?

No. Cap rates are based on net operating income before debt service, so they describe the property rather than the owner's financing.

### Can I use a cap rate to value my property?

It can be part of the picture for leased investment property, but only with a realistic NOI and genuinely comparable evidence. For many properties, a [full value review](/property-owners/) is more reliable. See [Preparing for a Commercial Property Value Review](/insights/prepare-commercial-property-value-review/).

### Practical takeaway

A cap rate links income to value. It is only as reliable as the income behind it and the comparability of the properties it comes from.
