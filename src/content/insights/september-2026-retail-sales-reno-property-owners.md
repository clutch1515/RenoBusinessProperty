I was looking at the September retail sales numbers, and something caught my attention. Nationally, sales were up. Pretty solid on the surface. But looking a little closer tells a different story, and I think it matters for [retail property owners](/property-owners/) here in Northern Nevada.

## What the September retail sales report actually showed

The U.S. Census Bureau’s [September 2026 advance report](https://www.census.gov/retail/marts/www/marts_current.pdf), released October 15, showed retail and food services sales increased **0.8% from August**. Gasoline station sales jumped **6.4%**. Take gasoline stations out of the equation, and the increase was just **0.2%**.

These are seasonally adjusted national estimates, and they can be revised. They also are not adjusted for price changes. Higher sales dollars do not necessarily mean people are buying more. They might just be paying more.

## Higher sales do not always mean a healthier tenant

For an owner, the distinction is pretty straightforward. A tenant can bring in more revenue while keeping less of it. If inventory, payroll, insurance, and other expenses rise faster than sales, there may be less money left to cover rent, even with a better number at the top of the income statement.

That does not mean your tenant is struggling. The national report cannot tell us that. But it does give us a reason to ask better questions before assuming sales growth supports a rent increase or an expansion.

I would want to know what is driving the tenant’s revenue. Are more customers coming through the door? Are they buying more each visit? Or did prices go up while customer traffic stayed the same? Those can look similar in a sales total, but they mean different things for the business.

## What this means for Reno and Sparks retail property owners

If you have a [tenant renewal coming up](/insights/commercial-tenant-renewal-questions-property-owners/), I would look at the business behind the rent payment. Payment history, current financials where available, and a conversation about operating costs will tell you more about that tenant than a national headline.

Market rent still matters. So do the cost of a vacancy, the work a replacement tenant might need, and how long it could take to get the space producing income again. A rent increase only helps if the full deal works.

For a buyer evaluating an occupied retail property, the same idea applies. Look closely at the rent roll, lease terms, tenant financial strength, and whether projected rent increases are realistic. I would be careful about building a purchase around higher future rents just because national retail sales increased.

## If you are looking for space, start with the full cost

For a business considering a new location, I would run the numbers against a conservative sales estimate. Include base rent, [NNN expenses](/insights/what-does-nnn-mean-commercial-lease/), utilities, insurance, and the cost to get the doors open. Then ask whether the location still works if sales take longer to build.

That is part of [comparing commercial spaces beyond the asking rent](/insights/compare-commercial-spaces-beyond-asking-rent/). A space can look affordable per square foot and still put too much pressure on the business.

The September report is national context, not evidence that Reno retail rents or local tenant sales moved by the same amount. For a specific property, we still need local lease comparisons and a clear look at the business occupying it.

My takeaway is pretty simple: higher sales do not always mean a healthier tenant or one that can afford higher rent. Before making a property decision, I think it is worth looking at what is left after the expenses are paid.
