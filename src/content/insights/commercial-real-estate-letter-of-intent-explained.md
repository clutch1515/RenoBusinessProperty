A letter of intent turns a conversation into a written outline of the main business terms. I use LOIs because they are simple, and I explain their limits up front, because that is where misunderstandings start.

Letters of intent, often called LOIs, are common in commercial leasing and sales throughout Northern Nevada. They let both sides confirm the major points before spending time and money on a full lease or purchase agreement. Understanding what an LOI does, and what it does not, helps tenants, buyers, and owners use it well.

## What an LOI does

- Summarizes the key business terms both sides intend to negotiate
- Identifies issues early, before legal drafting begins
- Creates a shared reference for attorneys preparing the final documents
- Signals seriousness and can set a timeline for next steps

## What an LOI usually does not do

Most LOIs state that the business terms are non-binding and that neither party is obligated until a final agreement is signed. That means either side can typically walk away or change positions before the lease or purchase agreement is executed. An LOI is not a lease, not a purchase contract, and not a guarantee that the deal will close.

## Provisions that may be binding

Some LOIs include specific provisions that are intended to be binding even when the business terms are not. Common examples include confidentiality, exclusivity or a period during which the owner will not negotiate with others, access for inspections, and who pays certain costs. The language matters, so have an attorney review any LOI that contains binding provisions.

## Typical terms in a lease LOI

- Premises, size, and how the area is measured
- Term, commencement, and rent start dates
- Base rent, increases, and the expense structure (for example, [NNN](/insights/what-does-nnn-mean-commercial-lease/))
- Improvements, allowances, and who manages construction
- Permitted use, signage, parking, and exclusives
- Renewal, expansion, or termination options
- Security deposit and guarantees
- Contingencies such as permits or financing

## Typical terms in a purchase LOI

- Price and deposit amounts
- Due diligence period and the information the seller will provide
- Financing contingency, if any
- Closing date and closing cost allocation
- Treatment of existing leases and tenant estoppels

## Common mistakes

- Leaving important issues for later, when leverage has shifted
- Assuming the LOI protects a position it does not actually protect
- Agreeing to exclusivity without a clear end date
- Not confirming that the intended use is allowed before signing
- Treating an LOI as a substitute for legal review of the final agreement

Use verification work, such as [confirming that the business use fits the location](/insights/verify-business-use-zoning-reno/) and [comparing total occupancy costs](/insights/compare-commercial-spaces-beyond-asking-rent/), to decide which points belong in the LOI.

## How I approach it

My rule of thumb: if a term would make either side walk away, it belongs in the LOI. Leaving it for the lease draft usually means negotiating it later, with less leverage.

## Common questions

### Can I back out after signing an LOI?

Usually yes, if the LOI states that it is non-binding, but any binding provisions such as confidentiality or exclusivity still apply. Read the document carefully and ask an attorney if you are unsure.

### How detailed should an LOI be?

Detailed enough to address the terms that would cause either side to walk away. Leaving major points vague often leads to delays or disputes during drafting.

### Does an LOI take a property off the market?

Only if it includes an exclusivity provision the owner agrees to. Otherwise the owner may continue to consider other proposals.

### Practical takeaway

An LOI is a roadmap, not the destination. Settle the major business terms, know which provisions are binding, and have the final agreement reviewed by qualified counsel.
