Something I've been thinking about lately is how much an exit cap rate can change the numbers on a commercial real estate investment.

When evaluating a property, it's easy to spend most of the time looking at today's income, financing costs, and projected rent growth. But the assumptions we're making about what that property will sell for in five or ten years can have just as much impact on the investment.

Here's a simple example.

## A hypothetical retail center in Reno/Sparks

Let's say a retail center in Sparks generates $150,000 in annual net operating income (NOI).

At a 6.5% cap rate, that property has an indicated value of approximately $2.31 million.

Now assume five years down the road, the property is generating the same income, but buyers are expecting a 7.25% cap rate.

That same property is now worth approximately $2.07 million.

|  | Initial valuation | Year 5 valuation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Annual NOI | $150,000 | $150,000 |
| Cap rate | 6.50% | 7.25% |
| Indicated value | $2,307,692 | $2,068,966 |

That's almost $240,000 in reduced indicated value, or roughly 10%, without anything changing at the property.

These are hypothetical numbers, not actual market cap rates for Sparks. The example simply shows how sensitive valuations can be to the assumptions we use.

## What about rent growth?

Obviously, there's more to underwriting than the cap rate.

Rents change, tenants leave, expenses go up, and hopefully NOI grows over time.

Let's say that same property's NOI increases 10% over those five years, from $150,000 to $165,000.

At a 7.25% exit cap rate, the property would be worth approximately $2.28 million.

That's considerably better than the $2.07 million valuation, and nearly back to where we started.

So income growth can offset some of the impact of rising cap rates. The question becomes how realistic that growth assumption is.

Are the leases structured to support it? Are there major tenant expirations coming up? Will operating costs eat into some of that growth?

Those details matter.

## Why this matters for Northern Nevada property owners

For retail and flex properties around Reno and Sparks, I'd pay particular attention to the lease terms, tenant strength, upcoming capital expenses, and the assumptions being made about future rental income.

Interest rates are part of the conversation too. They can influence financing costs and the returns buyers expect, although cap rates don't necessarily move in lockstep with Treasury yields.

A property with long-term tenants, predictable income, and limited capital needs may be viewed very differently from a similar building facing substantial vacancy or lease rollover.

There's no single exit cap rate that works for every property.

## What I'd look at before making a decision

When reviewing an investment, I think it's worth running more than one exit scenario.

What happens if you sell at the same cap rate you purchased at? What happens if that rate increases by 50 or 75 basis points?

Then I'd run those scenarios against realistic rent growth, tenant turnover, capital expenditures, and the expected loan balance.

The cap rate itself is only one assumption, but it can have a pretty significant impact on the overall return.

If a deal only works assuming you can sell at the same cap rate you bought it at, I'd want to take another look at those numbers.

For owners considering a sale or refinance, it also helps to start with a current rent roll, realistic NOI, and an understanding of what buyers are actually paying for comparable properties.
