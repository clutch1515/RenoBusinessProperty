Owning your building can be appealing: control, payments that build equity, and protection from rent increases. Leasing has real advantages too. When a business owner asks me which is better, my honest answer is that it depends on the business, its plans, and its capital.

Business owners in Reno, Sparks, and Northern Nevada who use their own space, often called owner-users, face this decision when a lease is expiring, the business is outgrowing its space, or a suitable building comes up for sale. This guide outlines the main trade-offs so the comparison is clear before talking with lenders, accountants, and brokers.

## Reasons businesses lease

- **Capital stays in the business.** No down payment, so cash remains available for equipment, inventory, staff, or growth.
- **Flexibility.** It is easier to expand, downsize, or relocate at the end of a lease term.
- **Predictable obligations.** Many building systems and major repairs may remain the landlord's responsibility, depending on the lease.
- **Less management.** The business can focus on its operations rather than on the building.

## Reasons businesses buy

- **Control.** The business decides on improvements, signage, and how long it stays.
- **Cost stability.** Fixed-rate financing can make occupancy costs more predictable over time.
- **Potential equity.** Loan payments reduce debt, and the property may gain value over time, though that is never guaranteed.
- **Possible income.** Extra space can sometimes be leased to another tenant.

## Compare the real costs

A fair comparison looks beyond the monthly payment. For leasing, include base rent, operating expenses, improvements, and expected increases. For buying, include the down payment, loan payments, property taxes, insurance, maintenance, capital repairs, and the opportunity cost of the cash invested. Compare both over the same period, and include a reasonable estimate of the building's value at the end. [How to Compare Commercial Spaces Beyond the Asking Rent](/insights/compare-commercial-spaces-beyond-asking-rent/) explains the leasing side in more detail.

## Questions that usually decide it

- How confident are you about your space needs over the next five to ten years?
- Would tying up capital in real estate slow the business down?
- Does the business need specialized improvements that are hard to justify in leased space?
- Is there a suitable building available for purchase in the right location?
- How do your accountant and lender view the options for your specific situation?

## Financing and tax considerations

Owner-user purchases are often financed with commercial loans, and some programs are designed specifically for businesses buying space they will occupy. Tax treatment differs significantly between rent and ownership. Talk with a qualified lender and tax adviser early, because their answers can change the comparison.

## Do not skip due diligence

Buying a building means taking on its condition, zoning, and history. Verify permitted use, inspect the building and systems, review title and any recorded restrictions, and consider environmental review where appropriate. [Before You Lease: Verify That Your Business Use Fits the Location](/insights/verify-business-use-zoning-reno/) covers the use questions, which apply to buyers as well.

## How I approach it

When I help an owner-user compare the two, I bring their accountant and lender into the conversation early. Their answers on financing and taxes can change the comparison more than any property detail.

## Common questions

### Is buying always cheaper over the long run?

Not necessarily. It depends on the price, financing, maintenance, capital repairs, how long you stay, and what the property is worth when you leave. Leasing can be the better financial choice, especially if the business needs flexibility.

### Can I lease now and buy later?

Yes. Some businesses lease while they grow and buy once their needs are stable. Occasionally a lease can include a purchase option, though that is negotiated case by case.

### What if I buy more space than I need?

Extra space can sometimes be leased to another business, which can offset costs. That also makes you a landlord, with the responsibilities that come with it.

### Practical takeaway

Lease when flexibility and capital matter most. Consider buying when your needs are stable, the right building is available, and the numbers work after every cost is included.
